01

What the atlas actually is

The single most important thing to understand before using Pickles for model testing: these are not spectra of stars. Each entry is a composite, averaged from several observed spectra of the same spectral type drawn from 15 different source libraries — Gunn & Stryker, Jacoby et al., Silva & Cornell, Sviderskiene, IUE-based UV sets, and others — then spliced, resampled to a uniform 5 Å grid and normalized to 1.0 at 5556 Å.

That construction drives everything else. There is no error array, because the noise is not a measurement of anything. There is no measured surface gravity or metallicity, because the composite has no single star behind it. The effective temperature attached to each entry is the temperature assigned to that spectral type in Table 2 of the paper, not a fit to the spectrum. And any peculiarity, rotation or reddening in the input stars has been averaged into the template rather than removed from it.

What you get in exchange is a clean, gap-free, uniformly calibrated grid across the whole HR diagram, with no noise, no slit losses and no instrumental systematics to model. For some questions that trade is exactly right.

Use Pickles when

  • You need a typical spectrum of a spectral type, not a specific star
  • You want the full type and luminosity grid, including supergiants
  • Noise would confuse the test and you want a smooth reference
  • You need a wide baseline — it reaches 1150 Å, and UVKLIB extends to 2.5 µm

Use NGSL or MILES when

  • You need measured Teff, log g and abundance for the object you are fitting
  • You need real error bars for a χ²
  • Resolution matters — both resolve the break better than R ~ 500
  • You need to know exactly which star you are looking at
02

The break across the atlas

I measured the Balmer discontinuity on all 131 templates with the same estimator used on NGSL, so the two libraries are directly comparable: both continua fit as upper envelopes and extrapolated to 3646 Å before ratioing, which cancels the underlying SED slope.

The measure behaves well for hot stars and badly for cool ones, and it is worth saying why rather than quietly trimming the axis. Below about 6000 K a star’s continuum is strongly curved and densely line-blanketed near 3600 Å, so a two-sided linear extrapolation stops isolating the discontinuity and starts tracking the slope. Uncorrected, M0III returns D = 2.52, which would rank it as the strongest “break” in the atlas; that is an artifact, not physics. The figure is therefore restricted to Teff > 6000 K.

  • Best-supported composite in the window. More real stars of this type sit in NGSL and MILES than for any other template near 10,000 K, so the template is both well determined and independently checkable.
  • It agrees with the individual stars. D = 0.957 against 0.969 for HD194453 and 0.980 for HD040573 — a 1–2% consistency between a ground-based composite and HST spectrophotometry of single stars.
  • The real prize is the sequence, not the single template. Pickles carries A0V, A0IV, A0III and A0I at 9550–9727 K — four luminosity classes at essentially one temperature.

Why that sequence matters here

The NGSL analysis ended on an unresolved caveat: HD194453 is typed A0III by SIMBAD but returns log g = 3.9 from the model fit, and since the Balmer break is gravity-sensitive, its luminosity class could not be pinned from one star. Pickles answers precisely that question, because it holds temperature nearly fixed and varies gravity alone.

The effect is real but modest and not monotonic in luminosity class: D runs 0.957 (V), 1.111 (IV), 0.993 (III), 0.976 (I). The supergiant is the clear outlier in shape rather than amplitude — its Balmer lines are dramatically narrower, and its blue continuum sits markedly higher, both classic low-gravity signatures visible directly in the figure below. Between dwarf, subgiant and giant, break amplitude alone spans only ~0.15 mag, so amplitude is a weak gravity diagnostic. Line width, not break depth, is what separates the classes.

04

Template against individual star

Overlaying the Pickles A0V composite on the NGSL recommendation puts both the agreement and the cost of compositing in one frame.

The compositing penalty is larger than the resolution penalty. Pickles samples the 3500–3800 Å break region with 59 pixels against NGSL’s 109, and resolves it at R ~ 500 against R ~ 939. But degrading NGSL to the Pickles grid would not reproduce the template, because line cores are additionally smeared by the velocity and rotation spread of the stars averaged into it. Fit the continuum shape of a Pickles template with confidence; do not fit its line profiles and infer anything about a single star’s gravity or rotation.

A note on the temperatures. Only 79 of the 131 templates carry a Teff, because the CDBS README tabulates temperatures for a named subset of types. The remainder — mostly the metal-weak and metal-rich F–K variants and intermediate subtypes — have spectra but no assigned temperature, and are excluded from every temperature-based figure here rather than being given an interpolated value.
